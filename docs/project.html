Project: A Model-Based Research Proposal

In this project you will work through the steps of a model-based research project: pose a question, write down a model that can answer it, argue that the data can identify the model, and show that an estimator recovers the answer. This is the approach we follow throughout the course.

You do not have to estimate anything on real data. Instead, you will show that your approach would work if the data were generated by your model.

The project has five sections, described below. The online textbook contains worked examples of each step.

Keep the model small

A model with two or three choices, a few state variables, and five to ten parameters is enough. A small model that you can solve, identify, and estimate in the time available is better than a large one that you cannot. You can discuss richer extensions in words.

1. The Research Question

State your research question in one or two sentences, and use one or two paragraphs to explain why it is of interest.

The question should ask for a number that a model can deliver. Most suitable questions take one of the following forms:

  • The effect of a policy that has never been implemented, or of an existing policy at a scale or in a population where it has not been observed. For example: how would employment among single mothers change if welfare benefits were subject to a 3-year lifetime limit instead of 5?
  • A welfare or distributional question that requires knowing preferences. For example: how much would families be willing to pay to remove a time limit?
  • A decomposition of an observed change into mechanisms. For example: how much of the decline in welfare use after 1996 was a behavioral response to time limits rather than a mechanical one?
  • A structural parameter with a clear policy interpretation, such as the Frisch elasticity of labor supply, the cost of entering a market, or the return to experience.

Your motivation should explain:

  1. Why the answer matters for policy, for welfare, or for understanding an economic mechanism.
  2. Why the question cannot be answered by a quasi-experimental research design alone. For example, the policy of interest may never have been observed, the answer may require extrapolating outside the support of the data, or it may require a welfare measure. The textbook chapter How and Why to Use Models discusses these cases.
  3. How the question relates to existing work. Two or three references are enough.

2. The Model

Write down a model that is rich enough to answer your question. The description should be complete enough that a reader could solve the model from it. Specify:

  • The agents and the choices they make.
  • Payoffs (utility or profit functions), including which parameters are to be estimated.
  • Constraints and the environment: budget constraints, policy rules, prices, technologies.
  • Timing and information: what agents know when they choose, what is uncertain, and how they form expectations.
  • State variables and their laws of motion, distinguishing endogenous states (affected by choices) from exogenous ones.
  • Unobservables: what agents observe that the econometrician does not, and whether these unobservables are iid, permanent (e.g. types), or evolving over time. This choice matters a great deal for Section 4.
  • The equilibrium concept.

Describe how the model is solved (for example by backward induction, as a fixed point, or as an equilibrium computation). You do not need to include code. Do include output that shows how the model behaves, such as choice probabilities as a function of a key state variable or simulated life-cycle profiles.

Finally, discuss any key modeling assumptions or simplifications and how they may affect your answer.

3. Answering the Question

Assume that you know the true parameters of the model. Describe the exact calculation that answers the question from Section 1. In particular, state:

  • The counterfactual: what changes in the environment (a policy parameter, a price, a constraint) and what is held fixed.
  • The population over which the effect is averaged, and the time periods.
  • The outcome: a choice probability, an average outcome, or a welfare measure such as a compensating variation.
  • Whether you need to solve for new prices or for other agents’ behavior.

Write the calculation as a formula where possible. Report the answer at the parameter values you use to simulate the model, and show how it varies with one or two key parameters. This shows which parameters the answer depends on most, and so which parameters Section 4 needs to treat most carefully.

The counterfactuals for the labor force participation model and the welfare time-limit model in the textbook are examples.

4. Data and Identification

Describe the data you have access to, and argue, as formally as you can, that your model is identified.

The data. You may describe real data that you have in mind or data that you imagine. State what is observed, for whom, and how often. Is it a cross-section or a panel, and how long is the panel? Are some outcomes observed only for some people (e.g. wages only for workers)? Is there policy variation across places or over time? Are any variables measured with error?

Different assumptions about the data let you compare approaches to identification. For example, you could ask what is identified from a single cross-section, what a panel adds, and what a policy change or an experiment adds. You are welcome to compare two or three such scenarios.

Whether the model is identified. Start from what the data reveal directly, such as choice probabilities conditional on observed states, the distribution of wages among workers, or transition probabilities. Then show that the parameters can be recovered from these objects. Ideally the argument is constructive: each parameter is written as an explicit function of features of the population distribution of the data. If you cannot do this for the full model, do it for a simplified version and explain what further assumptions the general case needs. State which normalizations you impose and whether they affect the answer to your question.

How the model is identified. Explain which variation in the data determines each parameter, especially the parameters that Section 3 showed matter most for the answer. Possible sources include:

  • exclusion restrictions (a variable that shifts one part of the model but not another);
  • policy variation across time, places, or groups;
  • the panel dimension (repeated observations on the same individual);
  • functional form or distributional assumptions.

Whether the identification is credible. Discuss whether the assumptions are plausible and defensible. Which assumption is most open to criticism, and how would the answer change if it failed? If identification relies mainly on functional form, discuss whether different data would provide a more convincing source of variation.

The textbook chapters on identification, on dynamic discrete choice, and on identification with unobserved heterogeneity contain examples of these arguments.

5. Estimation and Monte Carlo

Describe an estimator that follows your identification argument: if a parameter is identified by a particular moment or source of variation, the estimator should use it. Possible estimators include maximum likelihood with a nested solution of the model, minimum distance, (simulated) method of moments, indirect inference, CCP estimators, or a combination of these in several steps. Explain your choice, including any computational considerations.

Then use a Monte Carlo simulation to show that the estimator works:

  1. Choose “true” parameter values and simulate datasets that match your assumptions in Section 4: the same sample size, panel length, and observed variables.
  2. Estimate the model on each simulated dataset, using 100 to 500 replications, or fewer if the estimator is slow.
  3. Report the mean, bias, and standard deviation of the estimates across replications.
  4. Show that the estimator is consistent, for example by showing that bias and dispersion fall as the sample size increases.
  5. If you derived standard errors, compare them to the standard deviation of the estimates across replications.
  6. Use the estimates to compute the answer to your question from Section 3, and report how well the Monte Carlo recovers it. Recovering the answer is the main goal, not only recovering the parameters.

Optionally, use the Monte Carlo to study one of the threats discussed in Section 4. For example, simulate data from a model with a feature your estimator ignores, such as unobserved heterogeneity, and report how the answer changes.

Submission

Submit a single PDF by email. You do not need to include your code. Write for a reader who knows the material in this course but not your application.

Length

There is no minimum length for this project as long as you can effectively meet the requirements for each section. There is also no maximum length but submissions will be graded in part on their precision and conciseness.
